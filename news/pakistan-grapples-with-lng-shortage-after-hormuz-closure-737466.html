Pakistan Grapples with LNG Shortage After Hormuz Closure

By Umer Javed | 18 September 2026 | 5 min read
Photo: Markus Winkler / Pexels

Pakistan ranks among the worst affected by LNG supply cuts after the Strait of Hormuz was shut, threatening power stability and economic growth.

Pakistan ranks among the nations most affected by the sudden shortage of liquefied natural gas, following the closure of the Strait of Hormuz, according to a report by Dawn.

LNG has been a cornerstone of Pakistan’s energy mix for more than a decade. The country imported roughly 1.5 million tonnes annually, a figure that rose to 1.7 million tonnes in 2023 as the government sought to diversify its fuel sources. The majority of these supplies arrived through the Karachi terminal, where they are liquefied and stored before being dispatched to power plants across the country. The dependence on imported LNG is driven by the need to meet rising electricity demand, especially in the industrial and residential sectors.

The Strait of Hormuz, a narrow waterway that connects the Persian Gulf to the Arabian Sea, has historically been a vital artery for global LNG traffic. In early 2024, tensions in the region led to a temporary closure of the passage, which disrupted shipping lanes for several weeks. The incident forced vessels carrying LNG to reroute through longer, more expensive passages, thereby reducing the volume that could reach Pakistani ports.

The Scope of the Crisis

The immediate consequence of the closure was a sharp decline in LNG deliveries to Pakistan. According to the report, imports fell by approximately 25% in the first quarter of 2024, dropping from 1.7 million tonnes to around 1.2 million tonnes. This shortfall has put a strain on the power generation sector, where LNG-powered plants account for a significant share of the national electricity supply. With reduced fuel availability, operators have been forced to cut back on output, leading to intermittent load shedding across major cities.

Power plants that rely on LNG are particularly vulnerable because their fuel contracts are often short‑term and price‑sensitive. The sudden loss of supply has forced operators to resort to alternative fuels, such as coal or diesel, which are more expensive and less environmentally friendly. The shift has not only increased operating costs but also raised concerns about emissions and air quality in densely populated areas.

Impact on Power Generation and the Economy

Pakistan’s electricity deficit has widened as a result of the LNG shortage. The country has been battling chronic power shortages for years, and the latest crisis has exacerbated the situation. Power cuts have disrupted manufacturing processes, leading to reduced productivity and higher operational costs for businesses. In the agricultural sector, irrigation pumps that depend on reliable electricity have been affected, threatening crop yields during critical growing seasons.

Beyond the immediate power sector, the economic ramifications are far-reaching. Higher fuel costs translate into increased electricity prices for consumers, which in turn affect household budgets and inflation. The government’s ability to meet its fiscal commitments is also jeopardized, as the cost of importing alternative fuels strains the national budget. The shortage could also deter foreign investment, as investors weigh the reliability of the energy supply chain.

Government Response and Policy Measures

The government has acknowledged the severity of the situation and has begun to explore short‑term relief measures. Officials have confirmed that they are negotiating with LNG suppliers to secure emergency deliveries and are reviewing contractual terms to allow for more flexible sourcing. In addition, the Ministry of Energy has initiated a review of the national energy strategy to reduce dependence on imported fuels.

There have been calls for increased investment in domestic natural gas production, as well as in renewable energy projects such as solar and wind. The Ministry has also urged private sector participation in expanding the LNG terminal capacity to mitigate future disruptions. However, the construction of new infrastructure is a long‑term endeavour and will not address the immediate supply gap.

Regional Context and Diplomatic Dimensions

Pakistan is not the only country affected by the Hormuz closure. Several Gulf and Asian nations have reported similar shortages, prompting a collective reassessment of energy security. The incident has highlighted the vulnerability of over‑reliance on a single maritime corridor for critical imports. In this context, Pakistan has been engaging in diplomatic dialogues with key suppliers, seeking assurances that alternative shipping routes will remain open and that future disruptions can be avoided.

Diplomatic efforts have also focused on strengthening ties with alternative LNG suppliers outside the Persian Gulf. The government has expressed interest in negotiating contracts with countries in the Caribbean and Africa, where LNG production is on the rise. Diversifying supply sources is seen as a strategic priority to shield the nation from geopolitical shocks.

Potential Long-Term Solutions

Addressing the LNG shortage requires a multipronged approach. First, expanding domestic natural gas exploration could reduce import dependence, but this requires significant investment and time. Second, accelerating the development of renewable energy projects can provide a cleaner and more resilient power mix. Third, enhancing the efficiency of existing power plants will lower overall fuel demand, thereby easing the pressure on LNG imports.

In the medium term, Pakistan may consider establishing strategic LNG storage facilities to buffer against supply disruptions. These facilities would allow the country to build up reserves during periods of normal supply, which could then be tapped during crises. also, revising procurement policies to favor long‑term contracts with multiple suppliers could provide more stability.

Conclusion

The LNG shortage triggered by the closure of the Strait of Hormuz has exposed the fragility of Pakistan’s energy infrastructure. While the government is taking immediate steps to secure alternative supplies, the situation highlights the need for a comprehensive energy strategy that balances short‑term fixes with long‑term resilience. Ensuring a stable energy supply will be essential for sustaining economic growth and maintaining public confidence in the nation’s development trajectory.
