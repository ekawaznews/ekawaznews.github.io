A "super El Nino" event is expected to bring significant changes to global weather patterns, including Pakistan. Geo News has highlighted the potential impact of this unusually strong El Nino on the country’s climate.

What Is El Nino and How Does It Differ From a Super Event?

El Nino is part of the El Nino Southern Oscillation cycle, a natural fluctuation that involves the warming of sea surface temperatures in the central and eastern Pacific Ocean. This warming alters atmospheric circulation, affecting weather worldwide. A standard El Nino typically lasts six to nine months and can shift rainfall patterns, but a super El Nino extends the warm phase for up to a year or more and amplifies the associated atmospheric disturbances.

Global Impacts of a Super El Nino

When the El Nino phase intensifies, it can trigger extreme rainfall in some regions while causing severe drought in others. The altered jet stream can change storm tracks, leading to increased cyclone activity in the Indian Ocean and more intense winter storms in the Americas. Historically, super El Ninos have been linked to higher mortality rates in affected regions due to flooding, heat waves, and food shortages.

Projected Weather Patterns for Pakistan

For Pakistan, the super El Nino is projected to increase the intensity of the monsoon season, especially in the southern provinces of Sindh and Balochistan. Meteorological data suggest that rainfall totals could rise by 20 to 30 percent in these areas during the peak monsoon months of July and August. At the same time, the northern highlands may experience hotter temperatures and a shortened winter season, raising the risk of heat stress among the population.

Implications for Agriculture and Food Security

Farmers in Punjab and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa could face unpredictable rainfall patterns that may damage wheat and rice crops. Excessive moisture can lead to fungal diseases, while sudden dry spells may harm seedlings before they establish. The National Agricultural Research Council has warned that yield losses could reach up to 15 percent if irrigation systems fail to adapt to the changing climate conditions.

Flood Risks and Infrastructure Vulnerability

Flooding in low‑lying areas of Karachi, the Indus River basin, and the floodplains of the Ravi and Chenab rivers could damage roads, bridges, and power lines. The Pakistan Emergency Management Agency has identified more than 200 villages in Sindh alone that are at high risk of inundation. Recent studies show that the frequency of Category 3 or higher floods has increased by 25 percent over the past decade, a trend that a super El Nino may accelerate.

Government Preparedness Measures

The Ministry of Climate Change announced plans to strengthen early warning systems and improve water management infrastructure. The agency will expand the network of rain gauges and river level sensors across the country, allowing for more accurate forecasting. In addition, a new flood mitigation project is slated to begin in the Indus Delta, aiming to reinforce levees and improve drainage.

Community Actions and Public Awareness

Residents in flood‑prone districts are advised to stay updated on weather forecasts, secure homes by elevating essential items and reinforcing windows, and prepare emergency kits that include water, non‑perishable food, and first‑aid supplies. Local NGOs are coordinating with municipal authorities to conduct community drills and distribute educational materials on flood safety.

Scientific Outlook and Long‑Term Considerations

Scientists emphasize the importance of preparedness as the super El Nino unfolds over the next months. Climate models predict that the event could persist until early 2025, with its peak intensity expected in late 2024. The potential for extreme weather events highlights the need for integrated climate resilience strategies that combine infrastructure upgrades, community education, and agricultural adaptation.
