Strait of Hormuz Disruptions Threaten Global Trade, Pakistan's Economy

U
Umer Javed
Staff Reporter
| 🕒 19 September 2026 at 04:14 pm | 📖 5 min read
Share: 💬 WhatsApp 📘 Facebook ✈️ Telegram 🐦 Twitter
📷 Photo: İrfan Simsar / Pexels | ekawaznews.github.io

Disruptions in the Strait of Hormuz, alongside other geopolitical tensions affecting major shipping arteries, represent the worst threat to global trade in eight decades, the World Trade Organisation (WTO) chief, Ngozi Okonjo-Iweala, has stated. This assessment highlights a deepening crisis in international commerce, with profound and far-reaching implications for countries like Pakistan, which are heavily reliant on global supply chains for their energy needs and economic sustenance.

The Strait of Hormuz, a narrow waterway connecting the Persian Gulf with the Arabian Sea and the broader Indian Ocean, is an indispensable conduit for a substantial portion of the world's oil and liquefied natural gas (LNG) shipments. Its strategic location means that any instability or threat to shipping in this area has immediate and cascading effects across global energy markets, directly influencing prices and supply availability. For Pakistan, a nation heavily dependent on imported energy to fuel its economy and meet domestic demand, the situation in the Strait of Hormuz is a matter of critical national concern.

Pakistan imports a significant portion of its crude oil and petroleum products from Gulf countries, with these shipments predominantly transiting the Strait of Hormuz. Similarly, the country's growing reliance on LNG imports, primarily from Qatar, also positions this waterway as a lifeline for its energy security. Disruptions, whether due to direct attacks, increased security risks, or heightened geopolitical tensions in the region, translate directly into higher shipping costs, elevated insurance premiums, and potential delays in delivery. These factors inevitably push up the landed cost of energy in Pakistan, placing additional strain on its already fragile economy.

The direct consequence of higher imported energy costs is a spike in domestic prices for petrol, diesel, and electricity. This inflationary pressure affects every segment of Pakistani society, from individual households facing increased living expenses to industries grappling with higher operational costs. Manufacturing units, transport sectors, and agricultural producers all absorb these elevated energy prices, which can curtail production, reduce competitiveness of Pakistani goods, and ultimately slow down economic growth. The government, already struggling with fiscal challenges, also faces increased pressure to manage these inflationary trends and potentially provide subsidies, further burdening the national exchequer.

Beyond energy, the Strait of Hormuz is also a vital artery for Pakistan's broader international trade. A considerable volume of Pakistan's exports, including textiles, rice, surgical instruments, and sports goods, destined for the Middle East, Europe, and Africa, passes through this route. Conversely, numerous essential imports, such as machinery, chemicals, raw materials, and food items, also rely on these sea lanes. When these routes become riskier or more expensive, the competitiveness of Pakistani exports diminishes, and the cost of imported goods rises, contributing to an adverse balance of trade and potentially widening the current account deficit.

The WTO chief's warning about the unprecedented nature of the current threat, spanning 80 years, places the situation in a historical context that harks back to the disruptions experienced during and immediately after World War II. At that time, global trade patterns were severely fractured by conflict. Today, while the nature of the threats is different, the cumulative effect of various choke points being affected simultaneously is creating a similar level of systemic risk. The ongoing attacks in the Red Sea, impacting the Suez Canal route, combined with drought-induced restrictions in the Panama Canal, present a triple challenge to global maritime trade, with the Strait of Hormuz adding another layer of severe vulnerability.

Implications for Pakistan's Economic Stability

For Pakistan, the confluence of these global trade disruptions exacerbates existing economic vulnerabilities. The country has been grappling with high inflation, a depreciating currency, and efforts to stabilise its economy through various measures. The external shocks originating from disrupted trade routes threaten to undo some of the progress made and complicate future economic planning. Higher freight costs and extended transit times can lead to supply chain bottlenecks, creating scarcity of goods and further fueling inflation within the domestic market.

Economic analysts in Pakistan are closely monitoring the situation, recognising that prolonged instability in the Strait of Hormuz could lead to a sustained period of higher commodity prices and increased import bills. This scenario would put immense pressure on Pakistan's foreign exchange reserves, which are critical for financing essential imports and maintaining the stability of the rupee. Any significant depletion of these reserves could trigger further economic instability and hinder the country's ability to meet its international financial obligations.

The situation also highlights the need for Pakistan to consider long-term strategies for enhancing its energy security and diversifying its trade routes. While immediate solutions to global geopolitical challenges are complex, the present crisis highlights the importance of exploring alternative energy sources, strengthening domestic production capacities, and perhaps investing in infrastructure that could offer more resilient trade connectivity. The potential for the China-Pakistan Economic Corridor (CPEC) to offer alternative land-based trade routes, though primarily focused on connectivity with China, might be seen as part of a broader strategy to mitigate over-reliance on vulnerable sea lanes.

The severity of the WTO chief's statement serves as a stark reminder of the interconnectedness of the global economy and how geopolitical events in distant regions can have profound effects on national economies, even those far removed from the immediate conflict zones. For Pakistan, the disruptions in the Strait of Hormuz are not merely a global trade issue; they are a direct threat to its national economic stability, energy security, and the well-being of its citizens. Policymakers will need to remain vigilant and adaptable to mitigate the adverse impacts of these unprecedented global trade challenges.
