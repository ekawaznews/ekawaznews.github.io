Study Highlights Major Gaps in Environmental Lending Policies Among Pakistani Banks

Assessment of Commercial Lending Standards

Major commercial banking institutions in Pakistan have demonstrated very restricted progress when incorporating environmental considerations into their corporate financing operations, according to Dawn. The findings were documented in a comprehensive sector evaluation titled the 2025 Benchmark of Bank Sustainability Policies in Pakistan. This independent study was made public by Fair Finance Pakistan during side events organized around the World Bank and International Monetary Fund Annual Meetings hosted in Bangkok.

The evaluation focused on how financial entities translate internal ecological commitments into concrete prerequisites for the corporate clients and infrastructure initiatives they support. According to Dawn, the review highlighted that lending frameworks largely fail to demand rigorous green compliance from commercial borrowers. The gap between corporate rhetoric and practical lending decisions indicates that capital continues to flow toward projects without sufficient scrutiny regarding ecological damage or carbon output.

Observers of the financial sector have noted that lending practices within developing economies often prioritize short-term creditworthiness and borrower collateral over broader environmental risks. In Pakistan, commercial entities have historically faced diverse economic pressures, which can discourage lenders from imposing supplementary conditions on commercial loans. However, the evaluation suggests that omitting environmental standards leaves financial institutions exposed to risks that may emerge as global climate patterns change and international compliance standards tighten.

Detailed Findings on Environment and Ecology

According to Dawn, the findings revealed that climate change and biodiversity represent the weakest areas within the policies maintained by the surveyed banking institutions. The data compiled in the assessment established that positive policy coverage addressing borrowers and corporate projects reached only 13.8 per cent for climate change matters. This low proportion indicates that the overwhelming majority of commercial financing continues without explicit requirements designed to reduce emissions or protect climate stability.

The study also revealed a similar pattern regarding biological conservation. According to Dawn, positive policy coverage for biodiversity matters stood at 26.8 per cent among the assessed institutions. While this proportion is higher than the rating recorded for climate change, it still reflects a significant void in regulatory oversight. Commercial ventures that disrupt natural habitats or strain water bodies are frequently granted commercial credit without sufficient binding environmental stipulations.

The benchmark examination also indicated that 75.4 per cent of policy elements were highlighted in the findings concerning the operational coverage of the evaluated banks. Analysts suggest that such figures demonstrate a systemic reluctance across the domestic sector to formalize comprehensive environmental criteria. When policy elements lack mandatory implementation mechanisms, commercial bank branches may treat environmental criteria as voluntary guidelines rather than binding obligations during credit evaluation.

Institutions Evaluated in the Study

The assessment was carried out through an institutional partnership involving Fair Finance Pakistan, the Lahore University of Management Sciences, and Profundo. According to Dawn, the research initiative examined seven major financial institutions operating across the domestic market. The institutions named in the review were Habib Bank Ltd, United Bank Ltd, MCB Bank, the National Bank of Pakistan, Meezan Bank, Allied Bank, and Standard Chartered Pakistan.

These seven banking corporations collectively represent a substantial share of total commercial deposits and private lending across the country. Because these entities include state-backed lenders, conventional commercial institutions, an Islamic banking organization, and a multinational subsidiary, the sample reflects the broader financial architecture of the nation. The findings therefore offer a representative perspective on how the broader banking sector approaches environmental risks.

The inclusion of diverse institutional models shows that the absence of strict environmental criteria is not confined to one category of bank. Conventional institutions, state-affiliated organizations, and specialized lenders all displayed comparable limitations in their credit policies. Observers indicate that when the largest market players fail to adopt comprehensive standards, smaller financial institutions have little competitive incentive to introduce stringent requirements on their own commercial clients.

Relevance for the Domestic Economy

The absence of binding climate and biodiversity conditions within commercial lending carries direct implications for communities across Pakistan. The country remains highly exposed to environmental degradation, severe weather disturbances, and natural resource depletion. When financial institutions allocate capital without factoring in ecological harm, they inadvertently support commercial practices that can degrade water sources, reduce forest cover, and increase vulnerability to extreme weather.

Ordinary citizens often bear the brunt of unmonitored environmental destruction through polluted waterways, degraded air quality, and diminished agricultural yields. If commercial borrowers operate without incentives to reduce environmental impact, the resulting damage can disrupt local livelihoods and strain public health resources. The banking system functions as a gatekeeper of capital, meaning credit allocation decisions directly shape the physical environment that residents inhabit.

Financial analysts also warn that institutions neglecting climate risk could face long-term economic instability. If commercial borrowers operate assets that become unviable due to environmental degradation or shifting global policies, those borrowers may struggle to repay their loans. A high concentration of environmentally vulnerable assets on institutional balance sheets could create credit stress, ultimately affecting depositors and the wider economy.

Potential Repercussions for Borrowers

For corporate borrowers, the findings may signal changes in future borrowing conditions if domestic regulators or international partners demand reform. At present, businesses in carbon-intensive or environmentally disruptive industries appear to obtain funding with minimal green oversight. This dynamic allows companies to defer investments in cleaner machinery, waste treatment facilities, and renewable energy adoption.

However, this regulatory insulation may prove temporary. Global buyers and international export partners increasingly demand that their supply chains adhere to documented environmental standards. Pakistani exporters that rely on domestic commercial credit without meeting international ecological benchmarks could find themselves excluded from key foreign markets. Such developments could hurt industrial earnings and diminish national foreign exchange earnings over time.

also, domestic banking institutions that seek credit lines or correspondent relationships with international financial organizations could face growing questions regarding their sustainability portfolios. If foreign institutions impose stricter climate compliance rules on their local partners, domestic banks might be forced to alter their lending criteria abruptly, creating sudden compliance burdens for domestic commercial borrowers who are unprepared for new environmental requirements.

Future Outlook for Financial Governance

The publication of the benchmark assessment at an international forum in Bangkok highlights the scrutiny facing financial practices in emerging markets. According to Dawn, the release occurred alongside gatherings of international finance officials, placing the domestic banking sector under a broader spotlight. Moving forward, industry stakeholders will be observing whether bank leadership groups review internal lending manuals to address the gaps highlighted in the report.

Another area of focus is whether financial regulators will consider establishing mandatory environmental guidelines for project financing. Voluntary industry frameworks have so far yielded low policy coverage rates, as demonstrated by the figures recorded for climate and biodiversity oversight. Analysts suggest that without standardized, enforceable regulations, individual banks may hesitate to adopt stringent rules that could prompt corporate clients to move their accounts to less demanding competitors.

Civil society groups and academic researchers, including those involved in producing the benchmark report, are expected to continue tracking lending disclosures across the country. The pace at which the sector integrates environmental protections into commercial lending will determine whether private finance aids sustainable development or continues to fund activities that compound environmental vulnerabilities. How the banking industry responds in coming reporting cycles will provide a clearer picture of its genuine institutional direction.
