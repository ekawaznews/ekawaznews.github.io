HEC Should Not Rank Pakistan Universities, Dawn Editorial Stresses

By Umer Javed | 13 September 2026 | 3 min read
Photo: Anete Lusina / Pexels

Dawn argues that the Higher Education Commission should avoid ranking Pakistani universities, citing concerns over transparency and fairness. The editorial warns of potential harms to academic quality.

The Higher Education Commission should never rank Pakistan's universities, according to a recent editorial in Dawn. The piece argues that ranking exercises, as currently conducted, risk undermining the integrity of Pakistan’s higher education system and distort the priorities of institutions.

Background of the HEC

The Higher Education Commission (HEC) was established in 2002 to oversee and regulate universities and higher education institutions across Pakistan. Its mandate includes developing policies, allocating funding, and ensuring quality standards. Over the past two decades, the HEC has expanded its role to include accreditation, research funding, and international collaborations.

In 2018, the HEC launched a national university ranking system designed to provide a comparative assessment of institutions within the country. The initiative was intended to guide students, parents, and policymakers, and to foster a culture of accountability among universities.

The Ranking Initiative

The ranking framework was built on a set of indicators such as academic reputation, research output, faculty qualifications, infrastructure, and student satisfaction. Universities were assigned points for each category, and the cumulative score determined their position on the national leaderboard.

Early editions of the ranking drew mixed reactions. Some universities welcomed the visibility and used the rankings to benchmark their performance. Others criticized the methodology, arguing that it favored larger, well-funded institutions and did not adequately capture the diverse missions of smaller colleges.

Criticisms and Concerns

One of the primary criticisms highlighted in the Dawn editorial is the lack of transparency in the data collection process. Critics argue that the HEC does not disclose the raw data or the weightage assigned to each indicator, making it difficult for stakeholders to assess the fairness of the results.

Another concern is the potential for gaming the system. Several academics have expressed that universities might prioritize metrics that influence the rankings over genuine academic improvement. For example, increasing publication counts or hiring more foreign faculty could raise a score without necessarily enhancing student learning outcomes.

Political influence also emerges as a significant issue. Reports suggest that some universities with strong political ties receive favorable treatment in the allocation of resources, which may indirectly affect their ranking positions. The editorial warns that this could erode public trust in the HEC’s impartiality.

Impacts on Universities

The emphasis on ranking has led some institutions to shift focus from long-term academic goals to short-term metric improvements. Faculty members report increased pressure to publish in international journals, sometimes at the expense of teaching quality. Students, meanwhile, often choose universities based on ranking positions rather than program strengths or location suitability.

Smaller, specialized universities find themselves at a disadvantage. Their niche programs and community engagement efforts are not fully reflected in the ranking criteria, which tend to favor broad research output and large student populations. This dynamic risks widening the gap between elite universities and those serving regional or underprivileged communities.

Alternative Approaches

In light of these concerns, the editorial proposes a shift from ranking to a more holistic assessment model. Such a model would emphasize qualitative evaluations, peer reviews, and stakeholder feedback. By engaging faculty, students, alumni, and employers, the assessment could better capture the complex nature of higher education.

Another suggestion involves the creation of sectoral benchmarks. Instead of a single national leaderboard, universities could be grouped by discipline or region, allowing for more nuanced comparisons. This approach would help institutions identify specific areas for improvement without being penalized for structural differences.

Conclusion

The Dawn editorial concludes that the HEC’s attempt to rank universities, while well-intentioned, may do more harm than good. It calls for a reevaluation of the ranking methodology, increased transparency, and a move toward comprehensive quality assurance mechanisms that respect the diversity of Pakistan’s higher education.
