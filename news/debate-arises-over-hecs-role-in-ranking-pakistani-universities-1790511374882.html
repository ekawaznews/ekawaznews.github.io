Debate Arises Over HEC's Role in Ranking Pakistani Universities

U
Umer Javed

| 🕒 27 September 2026 at 05:16 pm | 📖 5 min read
Share: 💬 WhatsApp 📘 Facebook ✈️ Telegram 🐦 Twitter

A recent commentary published in Dawn has put forward the view that the Higher Education Commission (HEC) should never undertake the ranking of Pakistan's universities. This assertion brings to the forefront a significant debate regarding the methodology, purpose, and overall impact of institutional evaluations within the country's diverse higher education sector.

The HEC, established in 2002, serves as the primary regulatory body for higher education in Pakistan. Its mandate encompasses funding, regulating, and overseeing universities, aiming to promote quality education and research. Part of this oversight has historically included various forms of assessment and evaluation, sometimes culminating in public rankings intended to provide a benchmark for institutional performance and guide prospective students.

University rankings, both national and international, are generally conceived to offer a comparative analysis of higher education institutions based on a set of defined metrics. These metrics often include research output, faculty qualifications, student-to-faculty ratio, academic reputation, and internationalisation efforts. Proponents argue that rankings foster healthy competition among universities, encourage improvement, and provide valuable information for students, parents, and policymakers.

However, the utility and fairness of these ranking systems, particularly in a developing country like Pakistan, have frequently been subjects of considerable discussion. Critics often point out that global ranking methodologies are primarily designed for well-resourced, research-intensive universities in developed nations. Applying these same criteria without significant adaptation to institutions in Pakistan, which often operate under different priorities and resource constraints, can lead to skewed or unrepresentative outcomes.

One primary concern revolves around the methodology employed in such rankings. Many systems heavily weigh research publications in international journals, citations, and the number of PhD-qualified faculty members. While these are important indicators of academic strength, an exclusive focus can inadvertently disadvantage institutions that excel in undergraduate teaching, community engagement, or vocational training, which are equally vital for national development.

also, the financial resources available to universities play a substantial role in their ability to perform well on typical ranking metrics. Institutions with greater funding can attract top faculty, invest in state-of-the-art research facilities, and support extensive publication efforts. Public universities in Pakistan, especially those in less developed regions, often struggle with limited budgets, making it challenging to compete on a level playing field with their more affluent counterparts, or even with private institutions that have different funding models.

The Impact of Rankings on Institutional Focus

The pressure to improve a university's ranking can sometimes lead to an undesirable shift in institutional priorities. Instead of focusing on holistic educational development, improving teaching quality, or addressing specific local needs, universities might divert resources and efforts towards metrics that will boost their standing in a ranking table. This can include prioritising quantity of research over quality, or focusing on areas that generate publications rather than those with direct societal impact.

This reorientation can also affect curriculum development and student learning experiences. If faculty members are primarily evaluated based on their research output for ranking purposes, there might be less emphasis on pedagogical innovation or direct student mentorship. The core mission of educating a diverse student body and preparing them for the workforce or further studies might inadvertently take a secondary role.

also, the data collection and verification processes for rankings can be complex and susceptible to inaccuracies. Ensuring consistent, reliable, and verifiable data across a large number of diverse institutions in Pakistan presents its own set of challenges. Discrepancies in reporting or a lack of standardised data management systems can compromise the integrity and credibility of any resulting rankings.

Alternatives to Simple Ranking Systems

Given these challenges, the commentary suggesting the HEC should reconsider its approach to rankings invites a broader discussion on alternative or complementary methods for evaluating university performance. Instead of a single, composite rank, a more nuanced system could involve evaluating institutions against their stated missions and objectives.

For example, the HEC could develop a multi-dimensional framework that assesses institutions based on various categories of excellence. This might include separate evaluations for teaching quality, research impact, community service, graduate employability, and regional development contributions. Such an approach would acknowledge the diverse roles and strengths of different universities, allowing for a more equitable comparison.

Another valuable approach could be to focus on strong quality assurance mechanisms and accreditation processes. Rather than simply ranking, the HEC could concentrate on ensuring that all higher education institutions meet certain minimum standards of quality in their academic programs, faculty qualifications, infrastructure, and governance. This would ensure a baseline level of quality across the board, which is perhaps a more fundamental need for Pakistan's higher education system.

Capacity building initiatives could also form a central part of the HEC's strategy. By providing targeted support and resources to universities that are struggling in specific areas, the HEC could help uplift the overall standard of education rather than merely highlighting disparities through rankings. This could involve faculty training programs, grants for infrastructure development, and support for establishing research collaborations.

The Pakistani Context

Pakistan's higher education is characterised by significant regional disparities, varying levels of institutional maturity, and a wide spectrum of funding models. Some universities are decades old with established research traditions, while others are relatively new, focusing primarily on expanding access to higher education in underserved areas. A single ranking system struggles to accommodate this inherent diversity.

The nation's developmental needs also dictate that higher education institutions serve a broader purpose than just academic excellence in a narrow sense. Universities are expected to contribute to economic growth, social cohesion, and addressing local challenges such as climate change, public health, and poverty alleviation. Evaluation frameworks should recognise and reward these contributions.

Ultimately, the discussion around HEC's role in ranking universities reflects a deeper aspiration for a higher education system that is fair, effective, and truly serves the national interest. Moving beyond simplistic comparisons towards a more sophisticated understanding of institutional performance could empower universities to focus on their core missions and contribute more meaningfully to Pakistan's progress.
