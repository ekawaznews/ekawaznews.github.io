Pakistan's Middle Class Faces Quiet Economic Strain

By Umer Javed | 26 September 2026 | 4 min read
Photo: zarif amir / Pexels

Dawn's latest report highlights the silent struggle of Pakistan's middle class, as rising costs and job insecurity tighten the economic grip on families.

The Quiet Desperation of Pakistan’s Middle Class is the headline of a Dawn article that highlights the growing economic pressure on the middle class. The piece draws attention to the challenges that families in the middle income bracket are encountering across the country.

Defining the Middle Class

In Pakistan, the middle class is generally defined by households that earn between 40,000 and 100,000 rupees per month. These families often hold steady employment, own small businesses, or work in the public sector. They are the backbone of the nation’s consumer base, purchasing goods, paying for education and healthcare, and investing in housing and transportation. The article notes that this group has historically been seen as a stabilizing force in the economy.

Rising Costs of Living

Recent data indicate that the cost of essential items such as food, electricity, and transportation has increased steadily over the past year. According to reports, the price of staple foods has risen by 12 percent, while electricity bills have climbed by nearly 15 percent. These changes strain household budgets, forcing many families to cut back on discretionary spending or seek additional income sources.

When basic necessities become more expensive, the middle class often faces a dilemma: either reduce spending on non-essential items or find ways to increase their income. This pressure can lead to a cycle of debt accumulation and financial instability, especially for those who rely on monthly wages that do not keep pace with inflation.

Job Insecurity and Wage Stagnation

Employment security remains a concern for many middle‑class workers. In the private sector, contract work and temporary positions have become more common, leaving fewer employees with long‑term benefits. In the public sector, budget constraints have slowed the hiring of new staff and reduced the pace of salary revisions.

Wage growth has not matched the rate of inflation, meaning real earnings have slipped. This mismatch leaves families with less purchasing power and creates a sense of economic uncertainty that is often described as a quiet desperation.

Debt and Credit Pressure

To bridge the gap between income and expenses, many middle‑class households turn to credit. Consumer loans, credit cards, and micro‑finance products are increasingly used to cover day‑to‑day costs. While these instruments offer short‑term relief, they also increase long‑term financial obligations.

High debt levels can restrict future investment in education or property, limiting upward mobility. The article points out that this trend is growing, with more families reporting difficulty in meeting monthly repayment schedules.

Education and Healthcare Costs

Public schools in many provinces are overcrowded, and parents often pay for private tutoring or alternative institutions. The cost of tertiary education has risen sharply, with tuition fees at private universities doubling over the last decade. Families that can no longer afford these expenses must look for scholarships or defer education altogether.

Health care costs have also surged. While government hospitals offer low‑cost services, many middle‑class families prefer private clinics for quicker access. The out‑of‑pocket expenses for routine check‑ups and emergency care can add up quickly, further stressing household finances.

Government Response and Policy Measures

The government has announced several initiatives aimed at easing the burden on middle‑class families. Subsidies for fuel and electricity have been extended, and tax relief for small businesses has been introduced. However, the effectiveness of these measures remains under scrutiny.

Policy discussions continue on how to balance fiscal responsibility with social welfare. The article notes that a comprehensive strategy is needed to address the root causes of middle‑class distress, including wage growth, job security, and access to affordable education and health services.

Social Implications and Community Response

Beyond the economic data, the piece highlights the social impact of this quiet desperation. Families report increased stress, reduced leisure time, and a decline in overall life satisfaction. Community organizations and NGOs are stepping in to provide support through food banks, educational scholarships, and counseling services.

These grassroots efforts illustrate the resilience of Pakistani society, yet they also highlight the urgency of addressing the systemic issues that fuel middle‑class anxiety. The article concludes by calling for a collaborative approach that brings together government, private sector, and civil society to create sustainable solutions.

Looking Ahead

While the article offers a snapshot of the current situation, it also points to the need for ongoing monitoring and research. The middle class’s well‑being is integral to the nation’s overall economic health, and its challenges must be addressed with clear, actionable policies. The Dawn report serves as a reminder that the quiet desperation of many families is not a distant phenomenon but a pressing reality that requires immediate attention.
