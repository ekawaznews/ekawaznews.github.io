Afghan Graduates Challenge PMDC Return Orders in Lahore High Court

By Umer Javed | 18 September 2026 | 5 min read
Photo: Abdellah Benziane / Pexels

A growing number of Afghan medical graduates have approached the Lahore High Court, seeking to overturn return orders issued by the Pakistan Medical and Dental Council (PMDC). These petitions highlight ongoing challenges faced by foreign-qualified doctors seeking to regularize their professional status in Pakistan.

A new wave of Afghan medical graduates has filed petitions with the Lahore High Court (LHC), challenging return orders issued by the Pakistan Medical and Dental Council (PMDC). This action signals a continuing legal battle for foreign-qualified doctors who have pursued their education in Pakistan and now face obstacles to their professional integration.

The petitions, recently submitted to the LHC, represent a collective effort by these graduates to seek judicial intervention against directives that they contend are detrimental to their careers and future prospects. The specific nature of these return orders, while not explicitly detailed in the immediate context, generally pertains to regulations governing the recognition of foreign medical degrees and the subsequent pathways for registration and practice within Pakistan. Such orders often mandate students to return to their country of origin or fulfill additional requirements that were not initially part of their educational agreement or understanding.

Pakistan has historically been a significant destination for students from Afghanistan seeking higher education, particularly in fields like medicine. Universities across the country, especially in Punjab and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, have enrolled a considerable number of Afghan nationals over the decades. These students often invest substantial resources, both financial and personal, in pursuing their medical degrees, with the expectation of being able to practice medicine either in Pakistan or back in their home country, with their qualifications recognized internationally.

The Pakistan Medical and Dental Council is the primary regulatory body responsible for establishing and maintaining uniform standards of medical education and practice in Pakistan. Its mandate includes registering medical and dental practitioners, regulating medical and dental colleges, and ensuring the quality of healthcare services. The PMDC's decisions, therefore, carry significant weight for all medical professionals, including foreign graduates.

For Afghan graduates, the PMDC’s return orders present a complex set of challenges. Many of these individuals have spent years studying in Pakistan, adapting to its educational system and cultural environment. The prospect of having their degrees questioned or being compelled to leave the country without proper recognition of their qualifications can lead to severe professional and personal distress. It can also render years of hard work and financial investment effectively void, at least in the context of practicing in Pakistan.

The legal challenge mounted in the Lahore High Court typically involves a writ petition, a powerful tool in Pakistani jurisprudence allowing citizens to seek redress against actions of public bodies that they believe are unconstitutional, illegal, or beyond their jurisdiction. The graduates are likely arguing that the PMDC’s orders are arbitrary, discriminatory, or not in line with established regulations or international practices concerning medical education and recognition.

Sources familiar with the regulatory framework suggest that such orders from the PMDC might stem from various concerns. These could include issues related to the accreditation status of certain foreign institutions, discrepancies in curriculum standards, or administrative irregularities in the admission or examination processes. The PMDC’s role is to safeguard the quality of medical education and ensure that all practitioners meet a certain standard, for the safety and well-being of the public.

However, the petitioners are expected to contend that any such concerns should not unfairly penalize students who have diligently completed their studies in good faith. They may argue that if there were issues with the institutions or the educational pathway, these should have been identified and addressed by the regulatory bodies at an earlier stage, rather than impacting graduates after the completion of their degrees.

The ongoing legal actions highlight a broader issue concerning the recognition and integration of foreign-qualified medical professionals in Pakistan. While the country benefits from a diverse talent pool, the pathways for foreign graduates to obtain permanent registration and practice licenses can often be fraught with bureaucratic hurdles and evolving regulatory requirements. This situation is not unique to Afghan graduates but affects many international students who choose Pakistan for their higher education.

The Lahore High Court's involvement means that the issues will be thoroughly examined within the legal framework of Pakistan. The court will consider arguments from both the Afghan graduates, represented by their legal counsel, and the PMDC, which will present its rationale for issuing the return orders. The outcome of these petitions could set important precedents for how foreign medical qualifications are handled in Pakistan and impact future policies regarding international students.

The implications extend beyond the individual graduates. Such disputes can affect Pakistan's reputation as a regional hub for medical education. A clear and consistent policy for the recognition of foreign degrees is essential to attract international students and maintain confidence in the country's educational institutions. Uncertainty and prolonged legal battles can deter prospective students and strain diplomatic relations, particularly with neighboring countries like Afghanistan.

For many Afghan students, studying in Pakistan has been a pathway to a better future, offering opportunities for professional development that may be limited in their home country, especially given the socio-political conditions there. The ability to practice medicine is not only a matter of personal aspiration but also a significant contribution to healthcare systems, whether in Pakistan or Afghanistan.

The legal process in the LHC will involve detailed submissions, hearings, and potentially appeals. It is the strong judicial system in Pakistan that individuals, including foreign nationals, can seek remedies against administrative decisions of state bodies. The court's decision will be keenly awaited by the affected graduates, the PMDC, and the wider educational and medical communities.

The petitions highlight the need for greater clarity and transparency in the policies governing foreign medical graduates. A streamlined process that ensures quality while also facilitating the integration of qualified professionals is vital. This situation calls for a careful balance between upholding regulatory standards and providing fair opportunities for individuals who have invested significantly in their education.

The matter will likely proceed through several stages of legal scrutiny, with both sides presenting their cases before the court. The ultimate judgment will determine the fate of these Afghan medical graduates, impacting their ability to contribute their skills to the healthcare sector and potentially influencing future educational policies for foreign students in Pakistan.
