HEC Should Never Rank Pakistan’s Universities, Dawn Argues

By Umer Javed | 30 September 2026 | 4 min read
Photo: Anete Lusina / Pexels

A Dawn editorial urges the HEC to abandon university rankings, warning that such practices harm academic freedom and distort education quality. The piece calls for alternative evaluation methods that support Pakistan’s higher education landscape.

Dawn has argued that the Higher Education Commission (HEC) should never rank Pakistan’s universities, warning that the practice threatens academic autonomy and misrepresents educational quality.

The Debate Over University Rankings

The HEC introduced a ranking system several years ago to provide a benchmark for institutions across the country. According to reports, the criteria include research output, faculty qualifications, student enrollment and infrastructure. The initiative was designed to promote competition and improve standards. However, critics contend that the methodology fails to capture the full spectrum of academic excellence. Many observers note that the reliance on quantitative metrics overlooks qualitative aspects such as teaching effectiveness and community engagement.

The ranking framework has been implemented through periodic publications that list universities in descending order. These documents are widely cited by students, policymakers and international agencies. Some university officials have expressed concern that the rankings create pressure to prioritize publishable research over fundamental teaching responsibilities. A senior faculty member at Quaid‑i‑Azam University told reporters that the focus on citation counts diverts resources from essential curriculum development.

Impact on Academic Freedom and Quality

Education experts argue that ranking systems can undermine academic freedom by encouraging institutions to chase scores rather than pursue innovative scholarship. A former HEC chairman stated that the commission never intended to create a hierarchy that would dictate funding allocations. Nevertheless, the perception of a tiered system influences donor decisions and student choices. In some cases, less‑ranked colleges experience a decline in applications, which in turn affects their ability to retain quality staff.

The emphasis on rankings has also led to a proliferation of consultancy services that claim to boost institutional scores. According to reports, these services advise universities on how to manipulate data to improve their standing. Such practices raise ethical questions about the integrity of the evaluation process. A representative of a leading academic association told reporters that the commodification of rankings threatens the credibility of higher education altogether.

International comparisons reveal that many countries rely on multiple indicators rather than a single ranking list. In the United States, the Carnegie Classification and the U.S. News & World Report rankings coexist, offering complementary perspectives. Similarly, the United Kingdom uses the Research Assessment Exercise alongside teaching quality surveys. Pakistan’s current system, critics note, lacks the nuance found in these models and may not be suitable for a diverse higher education sector.

Alternative evaluation methods have been proposed to replace or supplement the existing ranking. Some experts suggest a balanced scorecard that includes student satisfaction, graduate employment rates and community outreach. Others advocate for peer review panels that assess teaching quality and research impact on a case‑by‑case basis. A policy analyst told reporters that such approaches would provide a more holistic view of institutional performance.

The editorial calls for the HEC to abandon the ranking exercise altogether and to develop a framework that supports collaborative improvement. It urges the commission to invest in capacity building, faculty development and infrastructure upgrades without creating a competitive hierarchy. According to the piece, the removal of rankings would allow universities to focus on their core missions without the distortion of score‑driven incentives.

Stakeholders across the education sector have responded to the proposal with mixed feelings. Some university chancellors have welcomed the idea, stating that it would free institutions from the pressure of climbing league tables. Others have warned that without a comparative metric, it may become difficult to allocate resources efficiently. A government official confirmed that the Ministry of Education is reviewing the HEC’s ranking policy and will consider the recommendations in upcoming budget discussions.

The debate over university rankings continues to shape policy discussions in Pakistan. As the nation seeks to elevate its higher education standards, the question remains whether a ranking‑free environment can foster genuine improvement or if some form of comparative assessment is essential. The outcome will influence how universities operate, how students choose institutions and how the international community perceives Pakistan’s academic.
