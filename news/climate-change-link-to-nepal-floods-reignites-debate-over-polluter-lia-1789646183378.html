Scientific analysis has confirmed that human-caused climate change significantly intensified the catastrophic floods that struck Nepal in late September, killing more than 240 people and causing widespread destruction across the Himalayan nation.

Attribution Study Confirms Climate Role

Researchers from World Weather Attribution, a global network of climate scientists, found that the extreme rainfall that triggered the floods was made at least 10 percent more intense by global warming. The study, released this week, examined the weather system that stalled over Nepal from September 26 to 28, dumping record rainfall in Kathmandu and surrounding districts.

The analysis compared the event with climate models simulating a world without the 1.3 degrees Celsius of warming caused by human activity since pre-industrial times. The results showed that such extreme rainfall events have become roughly 70 percent more likely in the current climate.

Devastation Across Nepal

The floods and landslides destroyed more than 15,000 homes and damaged critical infrastructure including highways, bridges, and hydropower plants. The Kathmandu Valley, home to over 3 million people, experienced its heaviest rainfall in at least 50 years. Rescue operations continued for weeks in remote mountain districts where entire villages were cut off.

According to Nepal's National Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Authority, economic losses exceed $150 million, a staggering sum for a country where per capita income remains below $1,500. The government has appealed for international assistance to support reconstruction efforts.

Loss and Damage Debate Intensifies

The attribution findings have reignited demands from vulnerable nations for the operationalization of the loss and damage fund agreed upon at COP28 in Dubai. Nepal, which contributes less than 0.1 percent of global greenhouse gas emissions, faces recurring climate disasters despite minimal historical responsibility.

"The science is clear. Countries that have emitted the most must pay for the damage they have caused," said a senior official at Nepal's Ministry of Forests and Environment, speaking on condition of anonymity. The official added that Nepal will present the attribution study as evidence at the upcoming COP29 summit in Baku.

Fund Remains Largely Empty

Despite the historic agreement at COP28 to establish a loss and damage fund, pledges from wealthy nations have totaled less than $700 million to date. The United States, the largest historical emitter, has not committed any funds. The European Union and several member states have pledged contributions, but disbursement mechanisms remain under negotiation.

Developing nations argue that the fund must provide grants, not loans, to avoid deepening debt crises in climate-vulnerable countries. They also demand that the fund be accessible quickly after disasters strike, without burdensome bureaucratic hurdles.

Legal Pressure Mounts

Beyond UN negotiations, legal avenues are gaining traction. Small island states have sought advisory opinions from the International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea and the International Court of Justice on state obligations regarding climate change. In May, the tribunal ruled that greenhouse gas emissions constitute marine pollution under international law, requiring states to take necessary measures to prevent harm.

Legal experts say attribution science strengthens the case for liability. "When science can quantify how much worse a disaster was because of emissions, it becomes harder for polluters to deny responsibility," said a climate law researcher at a Kathmandu-based think tank.

Regional Implications for South Asia

The Nepal floods highlight risks facing the entire Hindu Kush Himalaya region, often called the Third Pole. Glaciers are melting at accelerating rates, threatening water security for nearly 2 billion people downstream. Monsoon patterns are shifting, bringing more intense rainfall in shorter periods.

Pakistan, which suffered catastrophic floods in 2022 that killed over 1,700 people and caused $30 billion in damage, has also championed the loss and damage agenda. Pakistani officials have emphasized that regional cooperation on early warning systems and disaster preparedness must accompany financial mechanisms.

Adaptation Limits Reached

Experts warn that adaptation measures alone cannot address the scale of losses. Nepal has invested in early warning systems, flood barriers, and community-based disaster management. Yet the September rainfall exceeded design thresholds for much of this infrastructure.

"There are hard limits to adaptation," said a climate adaptation specialist at ICIMOD, the International Centre for Integrated Mountain Development based in Kathmandu. "When rainfall breaks all historical records, no amount of preparedness can prevent catastrophic damage."

Road to COP29

As negotiators prepare for COP29 in November, the loss and damage fund's governance structure remains unresolved. Key questions include which countries are eligible for funding, what types of losses are covered, and how the fund will be replenished over time.

Developing nations are pushing for a minimum annual floor of $100 billion for loss and damage alone, separate from existing climate finance commitments. Wealthy nations have resisted specific numerical targets, preferring voluntary contributions.

The Nepal attribution study adds to a growing body of evidence linking specific disasters to climate change. Similar analyses have connected warming to deadly heatwaves in India and Pakistan, floods in Libya, and wildfires in Canada and Australia. Each study strengthens the moral and potentially legal case for polluters to pay.

Domestic Response and Recovery

In Nepal, recovery efforts continue amid the onset of winter. Temporary shelters house thousands of displaced families in Kathmandu and hard-hit districts like Kavre, Sindhupalchok, and Dhading. The government has announced cash grants for families who lost homes, but distribution has been slow due to damage assessments and verification processes.

Local officials report that many affected communities are in areas prone to recurring landslides, raising questions about long-term resettlement. Geologists have identified hundreds of new landslide zones activated by the September rains, some threatening villages that were previously considered safe.

The monsoon season officially ended in early October, but meteorologists warn that post-monsoon weather systems can still bring heavy rainfall. The Department of Hydrology and Meteorology has urged continued vigilance in landslide-prone areas.

Global Responsibility

The Nepal floods represent a familiar pattern: those least responsible for climate change suffer its worst consequences. As attribution science advances, the gap between scientific certainty and political action becomes more difficult to justify.

For Nepal and similarly vulnerable nations, the question is no longer whether climate change worsens disasters. The question is whether the international community will translate that knowledge into financial responsibility before the next catastrophe strikes.
