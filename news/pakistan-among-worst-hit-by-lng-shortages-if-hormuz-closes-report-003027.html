Pakistan Among Worst-Hit by LNG Shortages if Hormuz Closes: Report

By Umer Javed | 18 September 2026 | 5 min read
Photo: Markus Winkler / Pexels

Pakistan faces severe LNG shortages and economic strain if the Strait of Hormuz is closed, according to a recent report by energy consultants Wood Mackenzie. The nation's significant reliance on imported liquefied natural gas makes it particularly vulnerable to disruptions in this vital shipping lane.

Pakistan is among the Asian nations most severely impacted by potential liquefied natural gas (LNG) shortages should the Strait of Hormuz be closed, a recent report by energy consultants Wood Mackenzie has confirmed. This assessment highlights the considerable vulnerability of Pakistan's energy sector and broader economy to geopolitical disruptions affecting global shipping lanes.

The Strait of Hormuz, a narrow waterway connecting the Persian Gulf to the Arabian Sea, is a critical chokepoint for global energy trade. It facilitates the passage of approximately one-fifth of the world's total LNG supply, primarily from major exporters such as Qatar and the United Arab Emirates. Any disruption to this strait would have immediate and far-reaching consequences for importing nations, particularly those with a high dependency on LNG for their energy needs.

Pakistan's Deepening Reliance on LNG Imports

Pakistan's energy has seen a growing reliance on imported LNG in recent years, making it acutely susceptible to price volatility and supply disruptions. The nation imports an estimated 3.6 million tonnes per annum (mtpa) of LNG through the Strait of Hormuz. This imported gas is a foundational component of Pakistan's energy mix, feeding into various critical sectors including power generation, fertilizer production, industrial operations, and the compressed natural gas (CNG) sector for transportation.

The shift towards LNG was partly driven by the depletion of indigenous gas reserves and a desire to bridge the widening demand-supply gap. While offering a cleaner alternative to furnace oil and enhancing energy security in theory, this reliance has inadvertently created a new set of vulnerabilities tied to international supply chains and geopolitical stability. The Wood Mackenzie report highlights how this dependency could quickly become a significant liability if the Hormuz route is compromised.

Economic Repercussions for a Vulnerable Economy

A closure of the Strait of Hormuz would trigger an immediate and substantial increase in spot LNG prices globally. For Pakistan, a nation already grappling with persistent economic challenges, this would translate into a significantly higher import bill. The increased cost of energy inputs would ripple through the economy, leading to elevated production costs for industries, higher electricity tariffs for consumers, and potentially exacerbating inflationary pressures.

Industries heavily reliant on gas, such as textiles, cement, and fertilizer manufacturing, could face severe operational challenges, including production cutbacks or even temporary shutdowns. This would not only impact economic output and employment but also affect Pakistan's export competitiveness. The agricultural sector, which depends on gas for fertilizer production, would also feel the strain, potentially impacting food security and rural livelihoods.

also, the power sector, where LNG plays a significant role in generation, would face substantial pressure. Higher fuel costs could lead to increased power generation costs, which would then be passed on to consumers, or result in greater government subsidies, further straining public finances. The prospect of more frequent and prolonged power outages, a recurring issue in Pakistan, would also become more likely, disrupting daily life and economic activity.

Global Impact and Regional Comparisons

The Wood Mackenzie report identified several other Asian nations that would face significant challenges alongside Pakistan. These include major economies like Japan, South Korea, India, and China, as well as developing nations such as Taiwan, Bangladesh, and Thailand. While these countries also rely on LNG, their economic scale, energy diversification, or strategic reserves might offer varying degrees of resilience compared to Pakistan.

Qatar, the world's largest LNG exporter, and the UAE are the primary suppliers whose shipments would be affected by a Hormuz closure. The inability to move these vast volumes of gas through the strait would necessitate exploring alternative, albeit highly complex and costly, shipping routes. One such alternative involves rerouting vessels around the Arabian Peninsula, adding significant transit time and expense. also, the massive Q-Max and Q-Flex LNG carriers, commonly used for Qatari exports, are too large to traverse the Suez Canal, limiting options for European-bound shipments and adding pressure on Asian markets.

The global LNG market is interconnected, and disruptions in one region inevitably impact others. Even if some supplies could be rerouted, the overall tightening of the market and the spike in prices would affect all importers, with those like Pakistan, having less purchasing power and fewer domestic alternatives, bearing a disproportionate burden.

Towards Long-Term Energy Security

The findings of this report serve as a stark reminder of the for Pakistan to enhance its long-term energy security strategy. While LNG imports have provided a quick solution to immediate energy shortfalls, the current geopolitical demands a more diversified and resilient approach. This could involve accelerating the exploration and development of indigenous oil and gas reserves, though this is a long-term endeavor with uncertain outcomes.

Investing in renewable energy sources, such as solar and wind power, offers a path towards reducing reliance on imported fossil fuels. While requiring substantial upfront investment and grid infrastructure upgrades, renewables offer insulation from international price fluctuations and geopolitical risks. Similarly, enhancing energy efficiency across all sectors, from industrial processes to household consumption, can play a significant role in managing demand and reducing overall import dependency.

Diversifying LNG supply sources and exploring different contractual arrangements, including long-term government-to-government deals, could also provide some stability. However, even these measures would not fully mitigate the impact of a complete closure of a vital chokepoint like the Strait of Hormuz. The report highlights the need for a comprehensive national energy policy that balances immediate needs with strategic foresight, aiming for a sustainable and secure energy future for Pakistan.

The potential for a Hormuz closure, though a hypothetical scenario, highlights the fragile nature of global energy supply chains and the particular challenges faced by nations like Pakistan. The report from Wood Mackenzie serves as a critical warning, urging policymakers to consider strong measures to safeguard the nation's energy future against external shocks, ensuring economic stability and continued development.
