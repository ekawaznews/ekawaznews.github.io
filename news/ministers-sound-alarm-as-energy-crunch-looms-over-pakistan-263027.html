Ministers Sound Alarm as Energy Crunch Looms Over Pakistan

By Umer Javed | 16 September 2026 | 5 min read
Photo: Hakan Kayahan / Pexels

Federal cabinet members warn of a deepening energy crisis as Gulf transit routes face disruption. The PM's fuel relief scheme is set for nationwide rollout, but ministers say Rs100 per litre is the limit the economy can bear.

The federal cabinet has openly sounded the alarm over Pakistan's escalating energy crisis, as disruptions in Gulf transit routes and soaring fuel prices compound pressures on an already strained economy. Senior ministers addressed the media on Tuesday, insisting that prudent planning has so far averted a far worse outcome, even as ordinary citizens brace for the impact of rising costs at the pump.

A Government Under Pressure

Members of the federal cabinet did not mince their words regarding the state of the country's energy sector. With Gulf energy transit routes effectively closed and fuel prices spiralling, the situation has become a matter of serious concern for those tasked with governing the country. The disruption in the Strait of Hormuz has directly affected supply lines, leaving Pakistan to rely more heavily on its limited domestic resources to keep the lights on.

Despite the severity of the situation, cabinet members maintained that careful and measured decision-making has prevented the crisis from deteriorating further. They pointed to steps already taken by the government to manage the shortfall and stabilise the situation, even while acknowledging that significant challenges remain ahead.

PM's Fuel Relief Scheme to Roll Out Nationwide

Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif's fuel relief scheme is set for a nationwide rollout from midnight, according to official confirmation. The move is aimed at providing immediate relief to consumers who have been reeling from sharply higher fuel prices across the country. The scheme, which had been tested in select regions, will now extend to all provinces.

However, the government has been clear that this relief comes with limits. Federal Minister for Planning Ahsan Iqbal stated that Rs100 relief per litre is all the economy can bear right now. He explained that anything beyond this threshold would place an unsustainable burden on the national exchequer and risk further destabilising the fiscal situation. The message from the government was direct: the relief is real, but it has defined boundaries.

Local Resources Keep the Grid Running

Federal Minister for Energy Awais Leghari confirmed that local resources have been instrumental in keeping the power grid operational amid the Hormuz disruption. He stated that despite the closure of key Gulf transit routes, the government has managed to maintain electricity supply through the utilisation of domestic energy reserves and alternative sourcing strategies.

Leghari's remarks carried weight at a time when energy analysts had raised concerns about the country's ability to sustain power generation without access to imported fuel. Pakistan's reliance on imported energy has long been a point of vulnerability, and the current disruption has exposed that weakness in stark terms. The minister's assurance, however, offered a measure of reassurance that domestic capacity is being fully tapped to bridge the gap.

No Smart Lockdown, Government Clarifies

The government has firmly denied rumours of a so-called smart lockdown being considered as a response to the energy crisis. As fuel costs spiral and pressure mounts on the power sector, speculation had begun to circulate in political and media circles about the possibility of restrictions being imposed to manage demand.

Ministers moved quickly to dismiss these rumours, stating that no such plan exists and that the government has no intention of imposing lockdown-type measures. The clarification came as a relief to many business owners and workers who feared that another round of restrictions could further damage an economy already struggling with inflation and declining growth.

The Economic Tightrope

The energy crisis in Pakistan is not happening in isolation. The country is grappling with high inflation, a depreciating rupee, and significant budget deficits. Energy subsidies have long been a contentious issue, with successive governments facing the difficult trade-off between providing relief to consumers and maintaining fiscal discipline.

The decision to cap fuel relief at Rs100 per litre reflects the narrow path the government is trying to walk. Providing too much subsidy risks alarming international financial institutions and further weakening investor confidence. Providing too little, however, could trigger public anger and deepen the cost-of-living crisis facing millions of households and small businesses.

According to reports, the government is also exploring ways to increase domestic energy production and reduce dependence on imports. These efforts include expanding the use of indigenous gas reserves and accelerating progress on renewable energy projects. While these long-term solutions are welcome, they do little to address the immediate pressures that consumers and industries are facing right now.

What Lies Ahead

The coming weeks will be critical for Pakistan's energy management. The nationwide rollout of the fuel relief scheme will be closely watched for its impact on both consumer sentiment and government finances. If the scheme is well-received, it could provide some breathing room for the administration. If it strains the budget excessively, it could lead to further difficult decisions down the line.

The disruption in Gulf transit routes remains a significant external factor. International developments in the region will directly affect the availability and cost of imported energy for Pakistan. Until those routes reopen or alternative supply chains are established, the country will continue to face elevated energy costs and the difficult choices that come with them.

For now, the government's message is one of cautious optimism. Ministers are projecting confidence that domestic resources and careful planning will see the country through the worst of the crisis. Whether that confidence proves justified will depend on events both within Pakistan and across the wider region. Citizens, for their part, will be watching closely to see whether the promised relief actually reaches them at the fuel pump and on their electricity bills.
